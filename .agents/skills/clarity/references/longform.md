# Authored long-form

Use this for essays, articles, newsletters, talks, narrative, fiction, and evocative writing. It does not override factual fidelity, supplied voice, medium, or brief.

## Reader and purpose

Picture a reader and distinguish what they know from what they need. Choose a takeaway appropriate to the register: an argument needs a supported position; an explanation needs an understandable mechanism; narrative needs events and a reason to continue. Do not force a thesis onto a piece whose job is wonder, remembrance, description, or story.

Each sentence should add a fact, image, mechanism, example, qualification, consequence, or useful change in pressure. Cut duplicated explanation and conclusions that exist only because the form seems to require one.

## Development and voice

Make paragraphs answer, complicate, show, or advance what came before. Name causal relations only when supported. For arguments, state the author's leaning and give the strongest real limitation enough room to matter.

Use the author's samples and supplied language to decide vocabulary, formality, person, punctuation, and rhythm. Protect real asides and unusual details; never insert them as proof of humanness.

After cutting, restore image, stance, rhythm, warmth, or a memorable line only when supported by the source. Ask or mark a question when the author-only material is missing.

Rewrite only within the authorized scope. Read the complete piece once before delivery and stop polishing when it works.
